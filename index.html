How deadly RNA viruses build themselves

We use X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM and biochemistry to see how measles, Nipah, Ebola and related viruses assemble at the cell membrane, and we use those structures to design ways to stop them.

Read about our research Join the lab

Research

Every enveloped virus has to gather its genome, its surface proteins and a piece of host membrane into one particle. In paramyxoviruses and filoviruses that job falls to the matrix protein. We study how it does it.

Building the matrix lattice

Matrix proteins pair into dimers and tile into an ordered layer just beneath the viral envelope. We determine the structures of these assemblies, from single dimers in crystals to whole lattices in virions by cryo-EM and cryo-electron tomography, to learn which contacts hold the lattice together and how it curves the membrane.

How lipids switch assembly on

We found that measles and Nipah virus matrix proteins bind specific membrane lipids, and that this binding is what drives them to polymerise. We are mapping how lipid recognition, membrane curvature and contacts with the viral glycoproteins and nucleocapsid are coordinated to produce an infectious particle.

Structure-guided antivirals

There are few treatments for these infections. Because matrix proteins are essential and conserved, their lattice contacts and lipid-binding pockets are drug targets. We run crystallographic fragment screens and functional assays to find molecules that stop viruses from assembling.

How we work

  • X-ray crystallography for atomic structures and ligand screening
  • Cryo-EM and cryo-ET for assemblies, lattices and intact virions
  • Membrane biochemistry with liposomes and lipid-binding assays
  • Cell biology and virology to test what the structures predict

People

Michael J. Norris, PhD

Principal investigator. Assistant Professor, Department of Biochemistry

Michael started the lab at the University of Toronto in April 2022. Before that he was a postdoctoral fellow with Erica Ollmann Saphire at Scripps Research and the La Jolla Institute for Immunology, where he worked on the structure and assembly of paramyxoviruses and filoviruses. He did his PhD at the University of Toronto with Richard Hegele and Theo Moraes, developing new therapies for respiratory syncytial virus, and his BSc and MSc at the University of Guelph.

He teaches BCH340, Proteins: From Structure to Function, and supervises research projects in BCH374, BCH473 and MGY280.
