Norris Lab

Department of Biochemistry, University of Toronto

We study how measles, Nipah, Ebola and related RNA viruses put themselves together at the cell membrane. Most of our work is structural (X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM), backed up by biochemistry and virology, and the long-term aim is to find drugs that stop these viruses from assembling.

Research

An enveloped virus has to bring its genome, its surface glycoproteins and a patch of host membrane together into one particle. In paramyxoviruses and filoviruses the matrix protein organises this. It forms dimers that tile into a lattice just under the plasma membrane, and that lattice decides where the virus buds, what it packages and what shape it takes. We want to understand the matrix well enough to break it.

The matrix lattice

We solve structures of matrix proteins alone and in assemblies, from crystal structures of dimers to cryo-EM and cryo-electron tomography of lattices inside virus-like particles and virions. Our questions are about contacts: which surfaces hold the lattice together, how it bends the membrane, and how it links to the glycoproteins above it and the nucleocapsid below.

Filovirus VP40 dimers form chains that stack into patches of 2D lattice beneath the membrane. Wan et al., eLife 2020

Lipids as the trigger

Measles and Nipah virus matrix proteins recognise phosphatidylserine and PI(4,5)P2 at the plasma membrane. Binding PI(4,5)P2 changes the protein's surface and sets off polymerisation and membrane deformation. The next question is how lipid recognition decides where and when assembly starts.

Norris et al., Science Advances 2022

Antivirals

There are no approved drugs for Nipah or Marburg virus disease. Matrix proteins are essential and well conserved within each family, so their lipid-binding sites and lattice contacts are attractive targets. We use crystallographic fragment screening to find compounds that bind them and then test whether those compounds block assembly and budding.

Werner et al., ACS Omega 2024 describes a crystallographic screen against Sudan virus VP40.

People

Michael Norris

Principal investigator, Assistant Professor

Michael started the lab in April 2022. Before that he was a postdoctoral fellow with Erica Ollmann Saphire at Scripps Research and the La Jolla Institute for Immunology, working on the structure and assembly of paramyxoviruses and filoviruses. He did his PhD at the University of Toronto with Richard Hegele and Theo Moraes on new therapies for respiratory syncytial virus, and his BSc and MSc at the University of Guelph. He teaches BCH340, Proteins: From Structure to Function.

Joining

Undergraduates can do a research project with us in the fall and winter terms through BCH374, BCH473 or MGY280. Email Michael with your transcript and a few lines on what you would like to work on.

Graduate students join through the Department of Biochemistry graduate program. Please get in touch before you apply so we can talk about projects and funding.

Postdoctoral fellows with a background in structural biology, virology or membrane biochemistry are welcome to write. Send a CV, a short note on what you want to work on, and the names of two referees.
